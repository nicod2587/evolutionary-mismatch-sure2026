Why is a social media feed so hard to put down? 
The marginal value theorem describes our foraging brain with a simple rule: stay and harvest a patch until its value falls below the environment's average. In the past, novel information reliably signalled value. Social media feeds now give us endless novelty with little benefit, leading to hours of wasted engagement. 
I asked whether a mind adapted for an ancestral environment gets trapped in a modern one. I modelled a forager as a two-state neuron inspired by brain regions that track opportunity cost. Each forager has two heritable traits: how much novelty contributes to perceived value, and its estimate of the environment's average value. I mapped the fitness of every trait combination as the environment moved from ancestral to modern, where novelty carries no information. Curiosity (prioritising novel patches) went unpunished in an ancestral world. As mismatch increased, a ceiling on curiosity appeared and kept falling, and evolved novelty seeking dropped about sevenfold. A mind adapted for chasing novelty went from thriving to failing, while less novelty-driven strategies continued to thrive. 
Compulsive internet foraging may be the same rule that kept us alive, now trusting signals that provide little value, as the internet arrived faster than evolution could keep up with. In the future, these predictions could be tested on browsing data.
